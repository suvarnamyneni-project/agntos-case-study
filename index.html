03

Problem

A paid deal lived in several systems, and in the notes between them.

How might we carry one deal from submission to the accounting hand-off on one platform, for every role, with every number traceable and every error visible?

The Transactions Re-imagined program aimed at an AI-native transaction platform for the US and Canada, meant to eventually replace TRX and SkySlope. Agents were to work in Agent Desk inside the agent platform. Staff were to work in Deal Desk, with Task Center kept as its own app for the work queue.

I framed the problem from the program archive, the war-room decodes, and agent feedback, and wrote the how-might-we the team designed against.

Agents could not see the work

Agents described an interface they disliked and required fields that did not belong on the deal. Some typed placeholder text to get past a screen. A rejected document did not ping them. MLS data was retyped. Logins were fragmented. The loudest complaint was the lack of one cross-transaction view of what they needed to do.

Staff pain sat in the gaps

Co-agent splits, advances, and transaction-coordinator fees arrived as free-text notes and were re-keyed. One settlement meant hopping between Task Center, Deal Desk, Doc AI, and SkySlope. A deal moved from submitted to paid across about 11 tool types. Status was hard to see, so agents asked support where the deal was.

Errors vanished after Settle

After Settle, an accounting variance or an Oracle failure could stay invisible to the analyst. The payment question ran from the agent, to support, to the analyst, to production support, to accounting. Canada’s closing analysts shared spreadsheets with finance.

05

End Users

Nine kinds of people touch a deal. A transaction coordinator is agent-hired. A transaction analyst is staff. The desks keep those two people apart.

Agent

Listing, buyer, team, referral

Five agent roles share the workspace: listing agent, buyer’s agent, team lead, team member, and referral agent. They need one view of what to do, a way to start from a document, and commission labelled as an estimate until a file is paid.

Agent-hired

Transaction coordinator

A TC is hired by the agent and is a payee on the file. They are not staff. Putting a coordinator on the staff role switcher was a gap the role check removed.

Staff

Transaction analyst

The analyst runs the desk: oldest-first queue, next action on every row, document blockers, financials, splits, the disbursement authorization, and settlement. Most of the happy path lives here.

Staff

Compliance reviewer

Documents waiting for a person. Tabs are Needs review, Escalated, and Approved. Approve sends the file to the analyst. Settlement on this desk is view-only.

Canada

Closing analyst

Canada’s closing analyst confirms funds, the settlement date, the payees, and who is settling. The file then becomes read-only and posts to accounting. Today that work is shared on spreadsheets with finance.

After close

Approver, broker, accounting, support

Approver, production support, accounting, broker and operations, post-close, customer support, and onboarding and training. They handle overrides, the accounting hand-off, the pipeline, and what happens when a settled file needs a correction.

Systems count as actors too: Doc AI, SkySlope, Task Center, the transaction calculator, the accounting service, Oracle, Enterprise, and the agent platform. Most failures sit in the hand-off between them.

User Personas

Representative personas. They stand in for the 19-role set, five agent and fourteen staff, that I used as shared language with product and operations. Role names are generalized.

LA

Listing agent

Stands in for the five agent roles: listing agent, buyer’s agent, team lead, team member, and referral agent. Goals: one cross-transaction view of what to do, a file that can start from a document, and commission labelled as an estimate until it is paid. Frustrations: an interface they disliked, required fields that did not belong, no ping when a document was rejected, MLS data retyped, fragmented logins. Needs: one Submit to Deal Desk, and the same timeline staff use.

TC

Transaction coordinator

Agent-hired, and a payee on the file. Goals: stay on the deal they were hired for, and get paid as a line on that file. Frustrations: being treated as staff, and fees that arrived as free-text notes and were re-keyed. Needs: a desk that keeps a TC apart from a TA. The coordinator came off the staff role switcher because a TC is not a TA.

TA

Transaction analyst

Staff, and the owner of the desk. Goals: take a submitted file through review, the disbursement authorization, and settlement without retyping. Frustrations: hopping between Task Center, Deal Desk, Doc AI, and SkySlope, and an error that goes quiet after Settle. Needs: oldest-first queue, the next action on every row, both values when sources disagree, and gates that say why Send DA or Mark disbursed is shut.

CR

Compliance reviewer

Goals: review the documents waiting for a person, then send the file on or back. Frustrations: exceptions that arrive without a source, and controls that are disabled with no reason. Needs: Needs review, Escalated, and Approved; Approve, Request changes, or Escalate with a reason and an audit line; settlement view-only on this desk.

CA

Closing analyst, Canada

Goals: settle the Canadian file and post it to accounting. Frustrations: a shared spreadsheet with finance, and US rules applied where Canada approves every override. Needs: confirm funds, the settlement date, the payees, and who is settling. The file then becomes read-only. The US and Canada stay different products under one shell.

BR

Broker and operations

Stands in for broker, operations, manager, approver, accounting, production support, post-close, customer support, and onboarding. Goals: see the pipeline through to Closed, Disbursed, and catch a variance or an Oracle failure. Frustrations: the role check found no oversight screen, and a payment question that ran through five people. Needs: the pipeline view, trigger-based US approvals, and a correction flow after a file is append-only.

Design Decisions

The rule was written before the picture. Estimates until paid, append-only after disbursed, and a TA who is not a TC decided more of the UI than the visual pass did.

Decision

One shell, many desks

Why. The same lifecycle runs under every module. The screen changes with the role. The file does not. A faster screen that sends someone elsewhere is a slower process.

Trade-off. Task Center stays a separate app. Deep links carry the role and the transaction. Business-unit context is required for multi-licensed agents and was not built in this prototype.

Decision

One chip, one timeline

Why. Status was the loudest gap. One chip per file, and the same timeline for the agent and for staff, so “where is my deal?” has one answer.

Trade-off. Status labels stay fixed. Passed and Approved stay different words, so an AI result is never written as a human approval.

Decision

Show both values. The person picks.

Why. Notes were the hidden integration. Review and choose shows both values with who entered each one, when, and the document page and section. Provenance is a chip on the value: Agent-provided, Extracted, TA-entered, or Deal notes. Financials open with “prefilled from the agent desk and extracts, edit only what changed.”

Trade-off. Which source wins by default is still an open decision. People do not retype, and the screen does not silently pick a winner.

Decision

An alert opens the fix

Why. Critical alerts gate Send DA and say why. An informational alert, such as a mentor minimum, stays on the file and does not block. The alert can open a guided fix in place. A workflow guide walks review alerts, confirm compensation, the DA, payments, settle, and post-settlement.

Trade-off. Send DA waits on every critical alert. The mentor fee percent is shown on the deal so an informational alert is visible without becoming a gate.

Decision

AI checks. A person decides.

Why. Doc AI meets the agent at creation. A missing signature is a hard block, with re-upload or an override recorded against a named person. Compliance is a person in the loop: Approve, Request changes, or Escalate, with a reason and an audit line. Gates fail closed. The payee total matches expected funds, close dates match, and variance is zero before the file can be marked disbursed.

Trade-off. The long-term map puts an AI reviewer in front, with auto-approval and auto-settle later, when clear-to-settle documents match and no flags remain. This prototype keeps the person in front. Doc AI is asynchronous, on the order of 30 seconds, so the screen needs a processing state.

Decision

Settled files are append-only

Why. A correction is its own flow: a reason, when to use it, the party at fault, and a description. Some reasons escalate to Post Close. A change log took the place of manual notes.

Trade-off. The real Settle step, a confirm modal, a payment date, and a demonstrated close-date block, is a next pass. So is a change log with old value, new value, and a reason on every line.

Decision

A TC is not a TA

Why. The combined prototype’s role check found an agent could land in staff UI on someone else’s deal, and that transaction-analyst controls were loose enough that Send DA could proceed with an open underpayment. The coordinator came off the staff role switcher. Every disabled Send control gained an inline reason.

Trade-off. The role switcher in the prototype is Agent, Transaction analyst, and Compliance analyst. Coordinator work stays on the file as a payee, not as a staff desk.

Decision

US triggers. Canada approves everything.

Why. The US and Canada are different products under one shell. In the US, a manager approves an override when a trigger says so. On Canadian deals, every override waits for approval. Disbursed waits on variance of zero, or an approved write-off.

Trade-off. Whether the two countries share a cap is still open. Only approval steps are unskippable. Workflows, core, repeatable, and attempt, replace checklists, so a sale attempt can be discarded while the listing persists.

Decision

The UI matches the API

Why. Supabase and row-level security, so the UI matches the API for each persona. It lives inside the agent platform shell. When a control is disabled, the screen says why.

Trade-off. Downstream accounting to Oracle, receivable then payable, and production support’s logs, retry, and restart are shown and owned by other teams.

Decision

Design the exception first

Why. The happy path is the rare file. Missing signatures, underpayments, and a failed posting are ordinary work, so they got a queue, an owner, and a reason. Integration failures become owned tasks in Task Center.

Trade-off. A fast prototype can still be wrong. Placeholder figures the model invented were treated as stand-ins. Every build was audited against the journey sheet.

UI Kit

The project’s own visual language, shown as a specimen. Calm surfaces, loud status. The page around it uses the shared case-study palette.

I own the agntOS design system. It is a monorepo. @agntos/tokens turns JSON into CSS variables, a TypeScript module, a Tailwind preset, and a contrast report. @agntos/ui is React and Storybook, with a light and dark toolbar and the accessibility addon.

Foundations are an ivory canvas, #f7f5f0, and a dark canvas, #0d0f14, with foreground #1b1f2a. Six hue pairs carry soft fills: accent, sky, sage, amber, clay, and violet. Violet is the AI hue. Type is Inter, Inter Tight for stats, Cormorant Garamond for display, and Geist Mono. Spacing is on a 4 pixel scale, controls use a 10 pixel radius, cards use 24, elevation is tinted, and focus is a 3 pixel ring.

When the checks pass, Approve sends the file to the analyst. Request changes sends it back. Exceptions from other systems arrive with their source named.

04 · The analyst prepares the money

The TA desk queue can be read oldest first. Filters follow the lifecycle, and Critical and Asked agent sit on the row with the age and the next action. Inside the file, a mismatch shows the agent-provided value and the extracted value before anyone acts. Financials arrive prefilled, with a source chip on each line. Splits keep the role they had in SkySlope. The long middle of this path, from queue to disbursed, including the rules the gates enforce, is written up on Deal Desk.

05 · DA, funds, and disbursement

The disbursement package lists payees, roles, amounts, and methods. Confirm stays shut until the payee total matches expected funds. Settlement shows the check, prefilled from the check copy, and variance reads Balanced only when actual equals expected. When funds balance and the dates match, the file can be marked disbursed. A toast confirms the hand-off to the accounting service, and the file turns read-only. It leaves the analyst’s queue.

06 · After settlement, Canada, and oversight

A settled file is append-only, so a correction is a separate request: a reason, when to use it, the party at fault, and a description. Some reasons escalate to Post Close. In the US, a manager approves an override when a trigger says so. On Canadian deals, every override waits for approval. Canada’s closing analyst confirms funds, the settlement date, the payees, and who is settling, and the file then becomes read-only and posts to accounting. Broker and operations see the pipeline through to Closed, Disbursed. I designed and prototyped this end-to-end path, and stress-tested it against the 14 journeys.

Outcome

Aimed at fewer screens and visible status. The score comes after launch.

The work is designed and prototyped. The intended outcomes are one desk, fewer screens and fewer system switches, less re-keying, status that every role can see, and errors that stay visible after a file says settled. Nothing in that list is measured yet. Impact will be measured after launch.

In the journey sheet, 38 of 57 steps were live in the settlement demo, 10 in progress, 5 proposed, and 4 still an open decision. Addresses, names, and dollar amounts on every screen are fictional demo data. This is not a shipped eXp product. Deeper studies: Deal Desk for settlement, and Agent Transactions for roles, permissions, and the back office.

Next design passes

  • The real Settle step: a confirm modal, a payment date, and a demonstrated close-date block.
  • An automatic, time-boxed post-settlement error flag, cleared by “manually handled.”
  • Manager approval as a Task Center task. US triggers, Canada approve-all.
  • The Canada closing-analyst flow taken from the spreadsheet walkthroughs.
  • Combined statement and check handling.
  • A change log with old value, new value, and a reason.
  • Per-document approval inside review tasks.
  • Admin and workflow configuration, with step tags for who can see a step.
  • Timed usability sessions with transaction analysts.

I keep the gap list and the open-question log, and I own the next design passes for settlement, approvals, and Canada.

Still open, on purpose

  • Which source wins when Doc AI and the deal data differ?
  • Do the US and Canada share a cap?
  • Is approval per document, or per stage?
  • How should out-of-order work sit against hard dependencies such as clear-to-settle and a W-9?

These stay on the table with owners, so the next pass has somewhere to start.

What I’ll carry to the next platform

01

Map the systems, not just the screens.

The failures I kept finding were hand-offs between Doc AI, SkySlope, Task Center, the accounting service, and Oracle. A calmer screen on top of that hop is still a hop.

02

Write the rule before the picture.

Estimates until paid, append-only after disbursed, and a TA who is not a TC. Those rules decided more of the UI than the visual pass did.

03

Design the exception first.

The happy path is the rare file. Missing signatures, underpayments, and a failed posting are ordinary work, so they got a queue, an owner, and a reason.

04

A fast prototype can be confidently wrong.

Claude and Cursor made a desk clickable the same day. The journey sheet was the check that kept a placeholder from becoming a business rule.

05

Leave the disagreement on the table.

Which source wins, whether the countries share a cap, per-document or stage approval. Named and owned, those questions move. Buried, they come back as rework.

These are my own lessons from designing the platform as its only product designer.
