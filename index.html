Overview

agntOS

One deal. Every role. One connected operating system.

Reimagining real estate transaction management through an AI-native, role-based platform that connects agents, transaction analysts, compliance teams and operations across the entire deal lifecycle.

Summary

Business problem

A deal that ended in payment crossed separate tools and separate roles. Fees lived in notes, status was hard to see, and an error after “Settled” could stay hidden from the person the agent calls.

User problem

Agents retyped listing data and could not see what to do next. Analysts rechecked the same figures by hand. “Where is my deal?” moved from the agent to support, to the analyst, to production support, to accounting.

Product solution

One platform, a desk for each role, and one shared timeline. Doc AI reads and checks. A person chooses, approves, or overrides. Money is provisional until it is paid.

My contribution

I was the sole designer on agntOS. I owned the product design end to end, including the design system, and I prototyped the desks in Claude Design.

Key capabilities

Start a deal from a document. Show the source of every figure. Fail closed, and say why. Give every exception an owner. Keep the US and Canada honest under one platform.

Status

Designed and prototyped, 2025–2026. Nothing on this page is a measured result.

Problem

Before agntOS, taking a deal from submitted to paid meant leaving the file behind at every hand-off.

  • Fragmented workflows

    The file moved across separate tools. Each hop was a place to retype, wait, or lose the thread.

  • Manual document work

    Staff compared dates and amounts between a document and the deal by hand. The archive described that comparing as the bulk of the work.

  • Repeated data entry

    Agents retyped listing data. Splits, advances, and coordinator fees arrived as notes and were typed in again.

  • Review delays

    A rejected document did not notify the agent. Required fields that did not apply to the deal were filled with stand-in text just to get past the screen.

  • Limited visibility

    The agent’s question, “Where is my deal?”, had no single answer. It was passed along a chain of people.

  • Compliance risk

    A missing signature, a mismatch, or a failed posting could sit outside the file the analyst was looking at.

  • Communication gaps

    Agents and transaction teams did not share one timeline. Canada’s closing analysts shared spreadsheets with finance.

  • Hidden failure

    After “Settled,” an accounting variance or a failed posting could stay invisible to the analyst the agent calls.

Expected cost of leaving it this way: more waiting between roles, more chances to mistype money, and compliance issues that surface late. That cost is not measured here.

Research

I synthesized the program archive, sat in war rooms, and logged the places the sources disagreed. I did not run interviews, shadowing, or usability tests. Sitting with analysts is the next step.

After close

  • Comparing was the work

    The archive kept describing the comparison of dates and amounts as the bulk of the effort. The judgment itself was the short part. Doc AI should do the comparing.

  • Notes were the integration

    Advances, fees, and splits lived in free text. Each line needs a source.

  • “Settled” hid the failure

    An exception after settlement needs an owner and a queue.

  • Money is provisional until it is paid

    Every figure before Closed (Paid) is labelled an estimate, with the reason it might move.

  • The US and Canada differ

    Canada uses a lawyer or conveyancer, firm and closed stages, and a closing analyst. Renaming a US screen would have misstated the work.

  • Systems fail at the hand-off

    Most of the failures I found in the archive sat between people and tools, not inside a single screen.

How I worked through the archive

I read discovery documents, war-room notes, the program document, and a settlement demo. I used Grok as a reading aid and checked every rule back to its source before it reached a screen.

I built an affinity map, the persona set, and a journey sheet. Each step is tagged by actor, system, automation, and build status. Open rules stay flagged.

I attended war rooms and a workshop, “Documents arrive, I can see my tasks.” That part was firsthand. The archive was still secondhand: I never sat with an analyst doing the work.

Questions I left open on purpose
  • Which source wins when Doc AI and the deal disagree.
  • Whether approval is per document or per stage.
  • Whether the US and Canada share a cap.
  • Who owns the deal once compliance signs off.
  • When a revised authorization is created.

I logged these instead of quietly picking a winner. There is no competitive analysis in this pass. The archive I worked from did not include one.

Strategy

How might we carry one deal from submission to the accounting hand-off on one platform, for every role, with every amount traceable and every error visible?

  • Doc AI reads and checks. A person decides.

    Approve, override, and settle are human actions. The prototype does not approve or settle on its own.

  • Show the source.

    Every value carries a provenance chip: Agent-provided, Extracted, Analyst-entered, or Deal notes.

  • Admit uncertainty.

    Review words are Passed, Suggestions, or Couldn’t review. Passed and Approved are never the same word.

  • Fail closed, and say how to open.

    A critical alert blocks and explains why. An informational alert stays visible and never blocks. A disabled control says why.

  • Design the exception first.

    A missing signature, an underpayment, or a failed posting is ordinary work, with a queue, an owner, and a reason.

  • One timeline, honest geography.

    The agent and staff see the same status. Canada keeps its own closing path.

How the roles connect

The agent works from Home, Inbox, Transactions, and Doc AI. Staff work the analyst desk, compliance, Canada, and broker and operations. The coordinator is not on the staff switcher. An agent who lands on someone else’s deal is sent back to their own. On a Canadian deal, the closing analyst approves financials, the disbursement package and every override together, then settles the file.

  1. AgentStart from a document, confirm what Doc AI read, submit, and follow the same timeline.
  2. AnalystWork the oldest file first. Clear missing information and mismatches without retyping.
  3. Document reviewDoc AI compares. A person picks the value, overrides, or sends the file on.
  4. SettlementThe payee package and the funds have to agree before anyone marks the file disbursed.

Agent experience

How can agents submit with less manual effort?

User problem

Agents filled required fields that did not apply, sometimes with stand-in text. They retyped listing data. A rejected document did not tell them. Their ask was one view of what they needed to do.

Existing experience

Submit, then wait. Status lived with support. The agent called to ask where the deal was.

User journey

Open Home, start from a document or from scratch, confirm what Doc AI extracted, fix or override a hard block, submit to Deal Desk, and watch the same timeline staff use.

Design exploration

I sketched the submit path before visual design: start from a document so details arrive as fields, show the source on each field, and create nothing until the agent confirms. The current screens are below. Earlier shell passes are in Prototype.

Key interactions and edge cases

  • A missing seller signature is a hard block. Create stays closed until re-upload or an override recorded against a named person.
  • Price is an estimate until the file is paid.
  • A Canadian file collects lawyer, jurisdiction, and closing date. There is a draft and a submitted version.
  • There is no separate agent document library beyond starting from a document.

Design rationale

The effort to remove is retyping and the phone call. Home shows what needs attention. Submit is one action. The timeline answers “where is my deal?” without a new status language.

Expected

Agents start from the document instead of retyping the listing, and they can see the file move without calling support. Not measured.

Transaction analyst

How can analysts prioritize and resolve exceptions efficiently?

User problem

One deal meant moving between the task queue, the old settlement screen, a separate document-reading tool, and the document system. Co-agent splits, advances, and coordinator fees arrived as notes and were typed in again.

Existing experience

The analyst rechecked every figure. After “Settled,” a variance or a failed posting could stay invisible, and the agent was often the first to notice.

User journey

Open the queue oldest first, read the next action, clear missing information, compare both sources on a mismatch, edit only what changed, and send the disbursement only when critical alerts are clear.

Design exploration

Before (my first prototype), an agent could land in the staff UI on someone else’s deal, and the coordinator sat on the staff role switcher. I removed the coordinator from that switcher and sent the agent back to their own deal. The queue below is the later prototype.

Key interactions and edge cases

  • Missing information is shown in context, with a way to ask the agent.
  • A compare view cites who entered each value and the document page and section. The analyst picks one. Which source wins by default is still open, so the prototype does not invent a winner.
  • An informational alert, such as a mentor fee under the minimum, explains the math and does not block.
  • A critical alert can open as a focused fix, instead of a hunt across tabs.
  • A failed accounting hand-off is an owned task with a traceable reference. Retry and restart are different actions. The task screenshot names an internal system, so it stays in the prototype and is not repeated on this page.

Design rationale

Priority is age plus the next action, not another inbox. Exceptions are tasks with owners. The analyst edits what changed and keeps the source chip, so the reason is not lost in a retype.

Expected

Analysts see what to do next and where each figure came from, and a failure after settlement has an owner. Not measured.

Document review

How can Doc AI reduce manual verification while keeping trust, accuracy, and human control?

User problem

A single “done” state would hide what the reader did not actually check. When the extracted value and the agent’s value disagreed, the analyst retyped one of them and the reason disappeared.

Existing experience

Staff rechecked every document by hand in a separate document-reading tool. Agents typed through required fields to move on.

User journey

Upload by starting from a document. Doc AI extracts what it can. The agent or analyst sees a mismatch, a missing signature, or a field that could not be reviewed, then a person re-uploads, picks a value, overrides, or approves.

Design exploration

I defined the states before the UI. The full set, and which ones exist only as labelled specimens, is in the next section. The screens here are the ones I could build from the prototype.

Key interactions and edge cases

  • Couldn’t review includes a document that cannot be read and a missing signature. The block names the way forward: re-upload or a named override.
  • Mismatch shows both values, who entered each, and the page and section. The choice stays visible and reversible.
  • Compliance on the review screen is Approve or Request changes, with a reason. Escalate is a queue route, not a third button on that screen.

Design rationale

Trust is the source, the uncertainty, and the person. Doc AI does not get a silent success state. Passed is not Approved.

Expected

People spend their attention on disagreements and blocks, not on rechecking every field, and every override names a person. Not measured.

Settlement and disbursement

How can the platform reduce financial errors and increase transparency?

User problem

Commission, splits, and fees were reconstructed from notes. The disbursement could move while money was still short, and a disabled action did not say why.

Existing experience

Before (my first prototype), sending the disbursement could proceed with an open underpayment, and compliance actions could be disabled with no reason. I closed those gates in the later prototype.

User journey

Open financials already filled from the agent desk and from extracts. Edit only what changed. Build the disbursement authorization, the package that says who gets paid. Confirm it only when the payee total matches expected funds. Mark disbursed only when the funds balance and the dates match.

Design exploration

An earlier back-office build held the same money questions in a denser record. The role desks below are the later prototype. Commission math lives on the financials and splits screens. There is no separate calculator screen.

Key interactions and edge cases

  • Each financial line keeps a source chip: Agent-provided, Extracted, Analyst-entered, or Deal notes.
  • Send waits on every critical alert, and a disabled Send says why: waiting on compliance, blockers still open, or already sent.
  • Mark disbursed requires the funds to balance and the dates to match. An approved write-off is not shown in the prototype.
  • A settled file is append-only after it is disbursed. A correction is its own flow, with a reason, and the original record stays.
  • On a Canadian deal, the closing analyst approves financials, the disbursement package and every override together, then settles the file.

Design rationale

Transparency is the source chip and the shared timeline. Error prevention is a gate that fails closed. The person still confirms the package.

Expected

A payee package cannot be confirmed while the totals disagree, and anyone looking at the file can see why a control is closed. Not measured.

AI assists. Humans stay in control.

Doc AI is the document reader in agntOS. It extracts and flags. A person picks the value, approves, or overrides, and the action is recorded.

  1. Upload
  2. Extraction
  3. Confidence
  4. Validation
  5. Human review
  6. Approval or correction
  7. Audit trail

One set of states covers that lifecycle: Processing, Extracted (high confidence or low confidence), Mismatch, Couldn’t review, and Override. A separate human step is Approval. Review words on a check are Passed, Suggestions, or Couldn’t review. Passed and Approved are never the same word.

Where the prototype already shows the state

  • Transparency

    Every extracted value can say where it came from. A disabled control says why it is closed.

  • Explainability

    A mismatch shows both values and the page and section. The person does not have to trust a single number on the screen.

  • Trust

    Couldn’t review is a real outcome. Hiding it would look like a pass.

  • Error prevention

    A critical alert blocks the disbursement. An informational alert does not.

  • Human in the loop

    Approve, override, and settle name a person. The prototype does not take those actions by itself.

  • Recovery

    Re-upload, ask the agent, request changes, or replay a failed hand-off. A correction after disbursement is appended. It does not erase the record.

Design system

I own the agntOS design system: tokens, components, keyboard behavior, names for screen readers, and tests. The screens below are the system in use. There is no separate Storybook or component doc in this repo.

  • Ink, for primary text
  • Paper, for the canvas
  • Violet, for AI
  • Sage, for a clear pass
  • Clay, for a block

Violet marks AI. Primary text stays high contrast in light and dark. Softer hues stay off body copy. A disabled control always says why.

What I checked, and what I have not

I audited my own prototype. That pass closed gates that would have let money move early, took the coordinator off the staff switcher, and made a disabled action say why.

I did not run usability tests with agents or analysts. Timed sessions with analysts are the next step, before another visual pass.

The product prototype is desktop web. This case study page adapts to smaller windows. I am not claiming the product screens were designed as a phone app.

Handoff is the clickable prototype, the design-system screens, and the Cursor brief. A fast prototype can be confidently wrong, so each build was checked again against the journey sheet. A stand-in figure was not allowed to become a business rule.

Outcomes

Designed capabilities

  • A deal can be followed from the agent’s home to disbursement on one timeline.
  • Doc AI shows what it read, what it couldn’t review, and where the sources disagree. A person chooses.
  • Money cannot move while a critical alert is open, and a disabled action says why.
  • A failure after settlement has an owner. A correction is appended after disbursement.

Expected product benefits

  • Less retyping, because a document can start the file and each line keeps its source.
  • Fewer places for a figure to change without a reason.
  • A shared answer to “where is my deal?”
  • Exceptions that are visible work, not a surprise phone call.

These are expectations. They are not measured.

Measured business outcomes

Not yet measured. agntOS is designed and prototyped.

Future opportunities

  • Decide which source wins when Doc AI and the deal disagree.
  • Decide whether approval is per document or per stage, and whether the US and Canada share a cap.
  • A flag after settlement that support can clear when a case was handled outside the desk, without rewriting the record. Not in the prototype.

Reflection

  1. Many roles, one file

    The coordinator is hired by the agent. The analyst is staff. Treating them as one person would have put the wrong work on the wrong desk.

  2. Enterprise workflows fail in the gaps

    A calmer screen on top of a hop is still a hop. I mapped the hand-offs before I trusted a layout.

  3. Automation and human control

    Couldn’t review mattered as much as Passed. Doc AI earns trust by showing the source and stopping when it should.

  4. Exceptions and compliance

    The happy path is the thin case. The missing signature, the short funds, and the failed posting are the product.

  5. Translating the archive

    I did not invent a rule when the sources disagreed. Open questions stayed on the table, in the UI where I could leave them undecided.

  6. What I would do next

    The archive and the war rooms were deep, but I never sat with an analyst doing the work. That is the session I would run before another visual pass.
